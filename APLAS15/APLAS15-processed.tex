\section{Decidable partiality}
\label{sec:partiality}

The bidirectional transformations discussed in this paper are ``decidably partial''.
By ``decidable partiality'' we mean that the transformations are actually total functions which wrap their results in the standard \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{M4d61796265}{\constructor{Maybe}}} datatype:

The result of a piece of successful computation is wrapped in the \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{J6a757374}{\constructor{just}}} constructor, while failed computation is represented by \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{N6e6f7468696e67}{\constructor{nothing}}}.
The behaviour of decidably partial functions is different from the usual partial functions as formalised in terms of complete partial orders (see, e.g., Gibbons~\cite{Gibbons-calculating-functional-programs}):
when defining the semantics of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{B426947554c}{\constructor{BiGUL}}}, we need to define programs that produce some result or fail respectively when a sub-program fails or produces some result, but this is not possible in the setting of complete partial orders (since such functions violate monotonicity, a necessary condition for computability).

We can choose to write our decidably partial bidirectional transformations directly as \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{M4d61796265}{\constructor{Maybe}}}-programs, but when proving their well-behavedness, we will need to analyse or establish how a piece of decidably partial computation succeeds or fails, and the task would be much easier if the computation steps leading to success or failure are reified as data.
(An example of such proofs is given in the definition of lens composition~\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_5f21945f}{\anonymous {\scalebox{.75}[1]{$\leftrightarrow$}}\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous }} in \autoref{sec:BX}.)

Informally:
A value of type \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}\;\identifier{A}} represents a piece of decidably partial computation that either successfully produces a value of type~\ensuremath{\identifier{A}} or fails.
The first two constructors \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R72657475726e}{\constructor{return}}} and \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_5f3e3e3d5f}{\anonymous {\bind }\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous }} represent the usual monadic operators~\cite{Moggi-monads,Wadler-essence}, and \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}} indicates failed computation.
We will also need error handling, which is represented by the \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361746368}{\constructor{catch}}} constructor.
The behaviour of our \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361746368}{\constructor{catch}}} is slightly different from the usual one: execution of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361746368}{\constructor{catch}}\;\identifier{mx}\;\identifier{f}\;\identifier{my}} binds the result of~\ensuremath{\identifier{mx}} to~\ensuremath{\identifier{f}} if \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}}~computes successfully, or goes to execution of~\ensuremath{\identifier{my}} if \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}}~fails to compute.
Also we will frequently need to ensure that a \ensuremath{\constructor{Bool}} value is \ensuremath{\constructor{true}} before continuing with the rest of a program, so the constructor \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{A6173736572745f7468656e5f}{\constructor{assert}\anonymous \constructor{then}\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous }} is included for convenience --- execution of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{A6173736572745f7468656e5f}{\constructor{assert}}\;\identifier{b}\;\protect\hyperlink{A6173736572745f7468656e5f}{\constructor{then}}\;\identifier{p}} becomes execution of~\ensuremath{\identifier{p}} when \ensuremath{\identifier{b}}~is \ensuremath{\constructor{true}}, or fails otherwise.

Formally, the meaning of the constructors of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}} is given by the interpreter \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R72756e506172}{\identifier{runPar}}\;{:}\;\{\mskip1.5mu \identifier{A}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}\mskip1.5mu\}\;\identifier{→}\;\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}\;\identifier{A}\;\identifier{→}\;\protect\hyperlink{M4d61796265}{\constructor{Maybe}}\;\identifier{A}} shown in \autoref{fig:runPar}.
We say that \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}\;{:}\;\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}\;\identifier{A}} computes to \ensuremath{\identifier{x}\;{:}\;\identifier{A}} exactly when \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R72756e506172}{\identifier{runPar}}\;\identifier{mx}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f22615f}{\identifier{≡}}\;\protect\hyperlink{J6a757374}{\constructor{just}}\;\identifier{x}}, and that \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}}~fails to compute exactly when \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R72756e506172}{\identifier{runPar}}\;\identifier{mx}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f22615f}{\identifier{≡}}\;\protect\hyperlink{N6e6f7468696e67}{\constructor{nothing}}}.
The first equality is what we aim to reify as data next.

Our reification of decidably partial computation as the datatype \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}} and \Agda's support of full dependent types allow us to compute for any \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}\;{:}\;\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}\;\identifier{A}} and \ensuremath{\identifier{x}\;{:}\;\identifier{A}} a type \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f21a65f}{\mathrel{\mapsto}}\;\identifier{x}} whose inhabitants explain how \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}}~computes to~\ensuremath{\identifier{x}} step by step.

\section{BiGUL and its lens semantics}
\label{sec:BiGUL}

Various bidirectional programming languages have been built based on the idea of lenses.
Such languages would contain a set of primitive lenses, like those we build by \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{I69736f2d6c656e73}{\identifier{iso}\text-\kern-.75\underscorespace\identifier{lens}}}, and a set of combinators which combine smaller lenses into larger ones, like what \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_5f21945f}{\anonymous {\scalebox{.75}[1]{$\leftrightarrow$}}\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous }}~does.
Usually these languages are designed to suggest that what they describe are \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} programs, but it has recently been argued~\cite{Fischer-essence-of-BX,Hu-putback-validity,Pacheco-putlenses,Pacheco-BiFluX} that the opposite \emph{putback-based} approach should be adopted instead.
The argument is based on the following theorem:
\begin{theorem}[putback is the essence]
Define, for every \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}},~\ensuremath{\identifier{my}\;{:}\;\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}\;\identifier{A}}, a type \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}\;\raisebox{\ht\strutbox}{\hypertarget{_2248}{}}\identifier{≈}\index{_2248@\ensuremath{\identifier{≈}}}\;\identifier{my}} to mean that \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f21a65f}{\mathrel{\mapsto}}\;\identifier{z}} if and only if \ensuremath{\identifier{my}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f21a65f}{\mathrel{\mapsto}}\;\identifier{z}} for any \ensuremath{\identifier{z}\;{:}\;\identifier{A}}.
Then for any \ensuremath{\identifier{S}},~\ensuremath{\identifier{V}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}} and two lenses \ensuremath{\identifier{l}},~\ensuremath{\identifier{r}\;{:}\;\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}\;\identifier{S}\;\identifier{V}} such that
\[ \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}\kern.6\underscorespace\text.\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}\;\identifier{l}\;\identifier{s}\;\identifier{v}\;\protect\hyperlink{_2248}{\identifier{≈}}\;\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}\kern.6\underscorespace\text.\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}\;\identifier{r}\;\identifier{s}\;\identifier{v}} \qquad\text{for all \ensuremath{\identifier{s}\;{:}\;\identifier{S}} and \ensuremath{\identifier{v}\;{:}\;\identifier{V}},} \]
we have
\[ \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}\kern.6\underscorespace\text.\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}\;\identifier{l}\;\identifier{s}\;\protect\hyperlink{_2248}{\identifier{≈}}\;\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}\kern.6\underscorespace\text.\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}\;\identifier{r}\;\identifier{s}} \qquad\text{for all \ensuremath{\identifier{s}\;{:}\;\identifier{S}}.} \]
\end{theorem}
The theorem tells us that, when designing a bidirectional combinator, if we can justify the design of its \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} component, then its \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} component needs no further justification, since there is no other \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} that can be paired with the same \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}}.
This is in sharp contrast with the \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}}-based approach, with which the designer needs to choose for each \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} one or a few possible \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} strategies to be paired with the \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}}.
This complicates the design as there would be multiple combinators with the same \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} semantics, distinguished only by differences in their \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} semantics --- this is rather counterintuitive as the programmer is supposed to think in terms of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} instead of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} when programming in \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}}-based languages.
Also it may well happen that, after the programmer describes a \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}}, the corresponding \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} synthesised by the language is not what the programmer expects.
All these problems simply do not exist for the putback-based approach.

Following the putback-based approach, our language \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{B426947554c}{\constructor{BiGUL}}} describes \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} functions, i.e., updates to a source using a view.
\autoref{fig:BiGUL} shows (a simplified version of) the top-level definition of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{B426947554c}{\constructor{BiGUL}}}.
In general, a \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{B426947554c}{\constructor{BiGUL}}} update proceeds by decomposing the source into several parts to be updated independently~(\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{U757064617465}{\constructor{update}}}; \autoref{sec:source-update}), and accordingly rearranging the view to match with these parts~(\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R7265617272}{\constructor{rearr}}}; \autoref{sec:view-rearrangement}), until the source and the view are reduced to the extent that basic operations can be applied~(\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R7265706c616365}{\constructor{replace}}}, \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}}, and \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{S736b6970}{\constructor{skip}}}; \autoref{sec:basic-lenses}).
When both the source and the view are lists, there is a powerful aligning operation for synchronising the two lists~(\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{A616c69676e}{\constructor{align}}}; \autoref{sec:list-alignment}).
And we can do case analyses on either the source~(\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361736553}{\constructor{caseS}}}; \autoref{sec:source-case}) or the view~(\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361736556}{\constructor{caseV}}}; \autoref{sec:view-case}) for describing more flexible update strategies.

\subsection{Replacing, failure, and skipping}
\label{sec:basic-lenses}

The three most basic operations are \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R7265706c616365}{\constructor{replace}}}, \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}}, and \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{S736b6970}{\constructor{skip}}}.
The \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R7265706c616365}{\constructor{replace}}} operation is applicable when the source and the view are of the same type, and its semantics is discarding the original source and returning the view as the updated source.
As mentioned in \autoref{sec:BX}, we can in fact derive its lens semantics from the identity isomorphism:
\begin{hscode}\SaveRestoreHook

The next construct \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361736553}{\constructor{caseS}}} is for doing case analysis on the source.
The basic idea of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361736553}{\constructor{caseS}}}'s semantics is to choose from a list of branches the first one that matches the source, and then execute the \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{B426947554c}{\constructor{BiGUL}}} statement of that branch.
It is, however, difficult to find a \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} to pair with this \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} semantics: the obvious \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} which selects the first branch that matches its source argument does not work, because it may well happen that the updated source produced by \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} matches a different branch from the original source, and hence in general we would have to guarantee \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P507574476574}{\identifier{PutGet}}} for \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} and \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} coming respectively from any two branches, which is unmanageable.
A more manageable solution is to insert a dynamic check at the end of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P707574}{\identifier{put}}} to ensure that the updated source must match the same branch as the original source, which is the solution adopted by Foster~et~al's concrete conditional lenses~\cite[Section~6.1]{Foster-lenses}.
This solution, however, is too restrictive to be useful in practice.

Our solution is to enhance \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{C6361736553}{\constructor{caseS}}} by adding a special kind of branches called \emph{adaptive} branches, in addition to \emph{normal} branches.
The lens semantics of the two kinds of branches are defined by the following datatype:

We must be more careful when it comes to the view, though:
As we mentioned in \autoref{sec:basic-lenses}, view information must be carefully managed and completely embedded into the source.
When the view matches a branch, the amount of view information that remains to be embedded into the source can actually be reduced.
For example, if there is a branch that matches an integer view which equals zero, then for this branch we may even perform no update on the source as long as we can guarantee that the subsequent \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} will choose this branch, in which case \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} can simply return zero --- the information that the view is zero is embedded implicitly in the control flow rather than explicitly in the updated source.

\subsection{Source update}
\label{sec:source-update}

A fundamental operation is performing updates to parts of the source.
We follow the approach taken by \textsc{Flux}~\cite{Cheney-FLUX}, which requires that updates must be independent --- that is, the same part of a source cannot be updated more than once --- to avoid the problem of sequencing updates.
It turns out that a pattern matching notation suits this task perfectly: pattern matching is arguably the most intuitive way to deconstruct a source, and we can specify apparently independent updates by writing them at the variable positions in a pattern.
The idea itself is straightforward; what follows, despite its slight complexity (especially if the reader is not familiar with dependently typed programming), is merely our strongly typed implementation of the idea in \Agda.

Since \Agda\ is fully dependently typed, we can naturally define patterns in a type-directed way such that nonsensical patterns are syntactically ruled out.
This task starts from the construction of a \emph{universe}~\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{U55}{\constructor{U}}}, i.e., a datatype whose inhabitants are interpreted as types.

\subsection{View rearrangement}
\label{sec:view-rearrangement}

The last operation \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R7265617272}{\constructor{rearr}}} is for rearranging the view to a specific shape suitable for subsequent updates.
For example, the \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{U757064617465}{\constructor{update}}} operation in \autoref{sec:source-update} demands that the view must be in a shape that matches the source pattern, and this is usually achieved by an outer \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R7265617272}{\constructor{rearr}}}.
In essence, \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{R7265617272}{\constructor{rearr}}} performs a piece of invertible computation (which loses no information), while its \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} semantics performs the inverse of the computation.
The invertible computation used is usually not complicated (hence the name ``rearrangement''), so instead of letting the programmer write general invertible functions (perhaps along with their inverses), we ask instead for syntactic descriptions of simple invertible computation, which are more restrictive but allow automatic inversion.

The kind of invertible computation we wish to express is a decomposition of an old view followed by an assembling of a new view using all components of the old view.
When it comes to decomposition, pattern matching should come to mind again; assembling can also be handled by pattern matching, as we have formulated pattern matching as an isomorphism in \autoref{sec:source-update}.
We thus require two patterns \ensuremath{\identifier{pat}\;{:}\;\protect\hyperlink{P5061747465726e}{\constructor{Pattern}}\;\identifier{D}} and \ensuremath{\identifier{pat'}\;{:}\;\protect\hyperlink{P5061747465726e}{\constructor{Pattern}}\;\identifier{E}}, the former for decomposing the old view of type \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_27e65f27e7}{\identifier{⟦}}\;\identifier{D}\;\protect\hyperlink{_27e65f27e7}{\identifier{⟧}}} and the latter for assembling the new view of type \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_27e65f27e7}{\identifier{⟦}}\;\identifier{E}\;\protect\hyperlink{_27e65f27e7}{\identifier{⟧}}}.
For the latter pattern \ensuremath{\identifier{pat'}} we also need to specify which component of the old view will be used as the value at each variable position.

\section*{Appendix: \Agda\ syntax}

An \Agda\ datatype is defined by listing all of its constructors along with their types in full.
A datatype can be \emph{parametrised}, as in the case of \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{M4d61796265}{\constructor{Maybe}}}, which has a parameter \ensuremath{\identifier{A}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}}, specified between the name of the datatype and the colon on the first line.
(\ensuremath{\constructor{Set}} is the type of all (small) types.)
Parameters can be referred to throughout a datatype definition.

Here the datatype \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}} is \emph{indexed} by \ensuremath{\constructor{Set}} (which appears to the right of the colon on the first line), meaning that \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}} is in fact a \ensuremath{\constructor{Set}}-indexed family of simultaneously defined types.\par
As for the constructors, let us look at the simplest case, \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}}:
The constructor \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}} constructs a value of type \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}\;\identifier{A}} for any \ensuremath{\identifier{A}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}}.
There is thus one argument to \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}}, namely \ensuremath{\identifier{A}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}}, and the type of \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}}'s result depends on the value of this argument (which happens to be a type).
This is an example of \emph{dependent function types}, whose result type can depend on the value of the argument.
Logically, dependent function types are read as universally quantified propositions --- the dependent function type \ensuremath{\{\mskip1.5mu \identifier{x}\;{:}\;\identifier{A}\mskip1.5mu\}\;\identifier{→}\;\identifier{B}\;\identifier{x}} is read as ``for all \ensuremath{\identifier{x}\;{:}\;\identifier{A}} the property~\ensuremath{\identifier{B}} holds for~\ensuremath{\identifier{x}}''.
(Ordinary function types, on the other hand, denote implications logically.)\par
Back to \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}}: most of the time \Agda\ can infer what \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}}'s argument should be based on how \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}} is used, so we make the argument \emph{implicit} by enclosing it in curly brackets.
(Explicit arguments, on the other hand, are enclosed in round brackets.)
We can then invoke \ensuremath{\constructor{fail}} without supplying the argument, leaving it for \Agda\ to infer.
We de-emphasise implicit arguments typographically when they cause visual annoyance, as in this case.\par
An \Agda\ name can be used as an infix (or, in general, mixfix) operator when the name contains underscores, in which case the underscores indicate where the arguments should go.
For example, we can apply \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_5f3e3e3d5f}{\anonymous {\bind }\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous }} to arguments \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}}~and~\ensuremath{\identifier{f}} by writing either \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_5f3e3e3d5f}{\anonymous {\bind }\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous }\;\identifier{mx}\;\identifier{f}} or \ensuremath{\identifier{mx}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f3e3e3d5f}{\bind }\;\identifier{f}}.

The type \ensuremath{\raisebox{\ht\strutbox}{\hypertarget{_22a4}{}}\identifier{⊤}\index{_22a4@\ensuremath{\identifier{⊤}}}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}} has exactly one constructor \ensuremath{\constructor{tt}\;{:}\;\protect\hyperlink{_22a4}{\identifier{⊤}}}, while the type \ensuremath{\raisebox{\ht\strutbox}{\hypertarget{_22a5}{}}\identifier{⊥}\index{_22a5@\ensuremath{\identifier{⊥}}}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}} has no inhabitant --- logically they are read as truth and falsity respectively.
The type family \ensuremath{\raisebox{\ht\strutbox}{\hypertarget{_5f22615f}{}}\anonymous \kern\underscorespace{\identifier{≡}}\anonymous \index{_5f22615f@\ensuremath{\anonymous \kern\underscorespace{\identifier{≡}}\anonymous }}\;{:}\;\{\mskip1.5mu \identifier{A}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}\mskip1.5mu\}\;\identifier{→}\;\identifier{A}\;\identifier{→}\;\identifier{A}\;\identifier{→}\;\constructor{Set}} is defined such that \ensuremath{\identifier{x}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f22615f}{\identifier{≡}}\;\identifier{y}} is inhabited if and only if \ensuremath{\identifier{x}}~and~\ensuremath{\identifier{y}} are equal.
The type former \ensuremath{\raisebox{\ht\strutbox}{\hypertarget{_5f228e5f}{}}\anonymous {\kern\underscorespace{\uplus}}\anonymous \index{_5f228e5f@\ensuremath{\anonymous {\kern\underscorespace{\uplus}}\anonymous }}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}\;\identifier{→}\;\constructor{Set}\;\identifier{→}\;\constructor{Set}} is disjoint union (disjunction) whose constructors are \ensuremath{\constructor{inj_1}\;{:}\;{}^{\color{gray}\{\mskip1.5mu \identifier{A}\;\identifier{B}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}\mskip1.5mu\}\;\identifier{→}}\;\identifier{A}\;\identifier{→}\;\identifier{A}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f228e5f}{\kern\underscorespace{\uplus}}\;\identifier{B}} and \ensuremath{\constructor{inj_2}\;{:}\;{}^{\color{gray}\{\mskip1.5mu \identifier{A}\;\identifier{B}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}\mskip1.5mu\}\;\identifier{→}}\;\identifier{B}\;\identifier{→}\;\identifier{A}\;\protect\hyperlink{_5f228e5f}{\kern\underscorespace{\uplus}}\;\identifier{B}}, and the type former \ensuremath{\raisebox{\ht\strutbox}{\hypertarget{_5fd75f}{}}\anonymous {{\times}}\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous \index{_5fd75f@\ensuremath{\anonymous {{\times}}\kern-\underscorespace\anonymous }}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}\;\identifier{→}\;\constructor{Set}\;\identifier{→}\;\constructor{Set}} is cartesian product (conjunction), whose constructor is~\ensuremath{\anonymous \kern\underscorespace\identifier{,}\anonymous }\kern1pt.
The symbol~`\ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{_5fd75f}{{\times}}}' is also overloaded to denote \emph{dependent pair types} --- the inhabitants of a dependent pair type \ensuremath{(\identifier{x}\;{:}\;\identifier{A})\;\protect\hyperlink{_5fd75f}{{\times}}\;\identifier{B}\;\identifier{x}} are pairs where the type of the second component depends on the value of the first component.
Logically, dependent pair types are read as existentially quantified propositions --- \ensuremath{(\identifier{x}\;{:}\;\identifier{A})\;\protect\hyperlink{_5fd75f}{{\times}}\;\identifier{B}\;\identifier{x}} is read as ``there exists \ensuremath{\identifier{x}\;{:}\;\identifier{A}} such that the property~\ensuremath{\identifier{B}} holds for~\ensuremath{\identifier{x}}''.\par
An underscore in a right-hand side expression instructs \Agda\ to infer what should be placed there.

An \Agda\ record is defined by listing the names and types of all its components, and a component type can depend on previous components.
To access a component of an inhabitant of a record type, we should use the corresponding component extraction function.
For example, \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}\kern.6\underscorespace\text.\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}\;{:}\;{}^{\color{gray}\{\mskip1.5mu \identifier{S}\;\identifier{V}\;{:}\;\constructor{Set}\mskip1.5mu\}\;\identifier{→}}\;\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}\;\identifier{S}\;\identifier{V}\;\identifier{→}\;\identifier{S}\;\identifier{→}\;\protect\hyperlink{P506172}{\constructor{Par}}\;\identifier{V}} is for extracting the \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{G676574}{\identifier{get}}} component of a \ensuremath{\protect\hyperlink{L4c656e73}{\constructor{Lens}}}.
