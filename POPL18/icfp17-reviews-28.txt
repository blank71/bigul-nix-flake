        Paper #28: An Axiomatic Basis for Bidirectional Programming
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

                      Overall merit: C. Weak paper, though I will not fight
                                        strongly against it
                         Confidence: Y. I am knowledgeable in this area,
                                        but not an expert

                         ===== Paper summary =====

Bidirectional programming languages tackle the *view update* problem: given a source *s* and a view *v*, resulting from executing a certain function *q* on *s*, if the view is updated into *v'*, the source *s* should be updated to an *s’* such that *q(s’) = v’*. Traditional bidirectional languages based on *asymmetric lenses* restrict in some form the set of updates allowed in order to automatically guarantee the desired update property. BiGUL is a bidirectional language that allows for arbitrary update policies, making verification of BiGUL programs highly non-trivial. The paper presents a hoare theory for BiGUL programs.

                      ===== Comments to authors =====

The paper does a contribution that might be relevant and interesting. Unfortunately, I was not convinced it was the case. As I see it, there are two main issues: there is not much justification for the direction of the work, and the presentation is hard for the not expert.

About the first issue, in the paper the justification for employing hoare triples is to allow (sort of) arbitrary updates in the views. But, even if we love hoare triples, they are a rather heavy mechanism for verification. Does the extra machinery justify the richer set of programs available? In the dissertation of Foster several additions were done to the original formulation of lenses, more interestingly with *resourceful lenses*, while keeping a nice level of abstraction with the desired properties. Why not keeping that direction?

About the second issue, the paper assumes certain familiarity with the BiGUL language. Since it departs considerably from work of Foster, I think a gentle introduction is needed. In the way the paper is organized, the set of language primitives seems arbitrary, and they do not make much sense at first sight. Perhaps showing up front some examples and the implementation of some primitives might help. For instance, I was able to better understand **skip** when I saw the implementation in the supplementary material.

                      Overall merit: B. OK paper, but I will not champion

                         ===== Paper summary =====

The paper presents a reasoning system for BiGUL---a putback-based
bidirectional language. The system is based on Hoare triples, adapted
specifically for Lens-like bidirectional transformations, and is shown
effective in reasoning about the input-output relationships of the
bidirectional programs. The system is formally defined and mechanical
proof are available as Agda programs.

                      ===== Comments to authors =====

I agree with the authors that the problem they are trying to solve is
an important one in bidirectional programming. As argued in the paper,
the standard well-behavedness properties is too weak for two reasons:
1) It is not sufficient for a precise specification of program
behaviours, leaving too much room for arbitrary decisions. 
2) Partiality of functions introduces a loophole, as well-behavedness can
be trivially satisfied through arbitrary “undefinedness”. In fact,
this problem is well-recognised within the bidirectional programming
community, and language designers go a long way to prevent it. For
example, the original Lens languages insisted on totality at the cost
of a very elaborate type system and restricted set of combinatory;
“generic point-free lenses” do not enforce totality, but made an
effort to make put more defined through more complicated combinators
and recursive patterns. In my opinion, the approach in this paper is
better. It separates programming and reasoning, allowing a reasonably
easy to use language with reasoning mechanisms on top.

The main reasons that I didn’t get more excited about this paper is
the lack of proper evaluation. It appears that most of the examples
presented in the paper are selected for easy explanation rather than
demonstrating the reach of the technique. The keyAlign example is
good, but there shall be more. In particular, to be fully convinced, I
need to see more examples of different types of pre- and
post-conditions that can be established for non-trivial programs,
though not necessarily the detailed derivations. The supplementary
material contains one more example “ReplaceAll”. But without
explanation, it is impossible to understand what it does.

Another weakness of the paper is the lack of discussion of related
work. I understand that the majority of the bidirectional languages
are get-based, which are different from BiGUL. But the goal of having
better control/specification of program behaviours is shared. In
addition to the lenses examples above, semantic bidirectionalization
in a sense also precisely specify the input/output domains, through
either static or dynamic (monadic) analysis. And the “shape
bidirectionalizer plug-ins” also provides a limited form of control. I
believe a discussion of how others attempted to solve the same
problem, but being less successfully, will not only make the paper
more complete, but also strengthen the main argument of the paper.

More detailed comments:

You mentioned that bidirectional has become largely synonymous with
lens-based approaches. Well, there are two interpretations of
“lens-based approaches”: 
1) The lens framework of asymmetric get/put
2) The combinatory-based language design. I can agree with the former,
but definitively not the later. There is a flourishing area of
bidirectionalization and other language designs that are not
combinator-based. I think you shall clarify this.

Theorem 3.8 is an important theorem, which is developed into the main
result later. But it is very difficult to understand what it says. You
mentioned that “a part of” the behaviour of get will be constrained.
What does this mean? What is the significance?

I am a bit confused by the consequence rule. You argued the need of
the non-standard rule with the example of “replace*replace”. But I
don’t understand why you will relax {_ (v,w) | v = w} to {(_,_)
(_,_)}, instead of {(_,_) (v,w) | v = w}? Will the product rule cease
to be applicable then? Also, if I understand correctly you are saying
{_ (v,w) | v = w} => {(_,_) (_,_)}. But how can this be right? The
consequence requires the source to be a pair, which is not implied by
the condition. Am I missing something?

In Section 4.4, I understand that the “b” part of the rearrV syntax is
“replace * skip …” in the keepHeight example. But it took me some time
to figure it out.

In Section 4.5, I do not understand the role of exit conditions. The
explanation given in the paper is to be “comparable with assertions in
reversible conditionals”. This can’t be all the reason, can it?

The subsequent example 4.3 has only a skip in the main branch. This is
convenient for the discussion of emb later on. But I will very much
prefer a more interesting example with a non-trivial main branch. In
my opinion, it is far more important to establish the usefulness of
the reasoning framework than to discuss the cute observations on emb.

In Section 7, you motivated the example by saying “Alignment is one of
the most important issues in synchronisation”. Well this might be
true, but your technique is no in any way tied to alignment, isn’t it?
So (over) emphasising like this may mislead people.

                      Overall merit: D. Reject

                         ===== Paper summary =====

Bidirectional programming attempts to solve the problem of writing
synchronisation code that reconciles differences between two versions
of some data. Lenses are a popular formalism that captures some
properties of how a 'good' reconciliation procedure should
behave. However, these properties do not capture everything that one
might want to know about some reconciliation procedure. For example,
how missing data is dealt with.

This paper proposes a solution to this problem by formulating a
Hoare-style logic for a bidirectional programming language BiGul. All
BiGul programs describe (partial) lenses. The idea is that the pre-
and post-conditions proved for some BiGul program describe in more
detail how the reconciliation is carried out. The main example given
is a key-based list alignment synchroniser that is shown to have
certain properties via the logic.

The logic proved sound in Agda.

                      ===== Comments to authors =====

While this paper tackles an interesting problem, and appears to have
some deep insights into bidirectional programming, I think that the
presentation is far too inaccessible to be accepted in its present
form.


Things that I liked about this paper

- I think that the problem being addressed is important, and that the
  authors seem to have several deep insights into the structure of
  bidirectional programs, and how to reason about them.


Things that I think could be improved in this paper

- At the sentence level, the writing is very clear. However, the paper
  left me completely lacking an intuitive handle on what BiGul
  programs mean, and how I might go about writing one
  myself. Specifically:

     1. What are the typing rules of BiGul? The Hoare logic rules in
        Figure 1 implicitly use the typing of BiGul programs to make
        sure everything makes sense, but the reader who is not
        familiar with BiGul (which is going to be pretty much
        everyone) won't have these types to hand.

     2. What is the semantics of BiGul? Definition 2.1 states that
        every BiGul program has a 'put' and a 'get' interpretation,
        but doesn't say what they are! (and even states that a
        previously published version is different!) I think I
        understand the semantics of 'fail', 'replace', 'skip', '*',
        and (possibly) 'rearrV' and 'rearrS', but I am lost on the
        interpretation of 'case'. What does it mean for the process to
        be "rerun" (Page 11, line 32). How does this affect
        termination -- the types given in Definition 2.1 and the
        references to Agda would imply that the 'get' and 'put'
        functions are total?
        
  I'm afraid that the lack of an introduction to BiGul and its
  semantics made it very difficult to for me to evaluate the rest of
  the paper. I think that the problem being tackled is important, but
  this presentation is not yet ready.

- If the goal is to give better specifications to bidirectional
  programs that specify the strategies they use for reconciliation,
  then there is no discussion in the paper on how to interpret the
  Hoare triples from this point of view. A discussion of how the
  different strategies listed in lines 10-20 on page 2 are expressed
  as Hoare-triples would be very welcome.

Other comments:

- The consequence rule. I don't like the expression of this rule
  because it is non-compositional for proofs in the sense that, in the
  listing presentation used in the paper, it reaches back to an
  arbitrary point for the 'T'. I think a better way of expressing this
  would be to have a normal consequence rule, and a separation
  logic-style frame rule:

         {R}b{R'}  ===> {R \cap S}b{R' \cap <_ s v | S s v>}

- Also, I don't see the product rule (Section 4.2) as Separation logic
  style frame rule because the underlying type of 'store' changes.

                         ===== Paper summary =====

Bidirectional programming aims to allow users to write programs
that maintain consistency between two representations of a value,
with a change in one representation being automatically translated
into a corresponding change in the other value, in either direction.
This paper develops a Hoare-style logic for reasoning about the
behaviour of bidirectional programs in the BIGUL language.  The
paper presents the new logic in a semi-formal way, but the theory
has been fully formalised in Agda.  A range of examples that
illustrate the use of the new logic are provided.

                      ===== Comments to authors =====

General comments:

This paper is beautifully written!  I'm not from the bidirectional
programming community, but found it accessible and interesting.
I enjoyed the light tone, and didn't find a single typo.  If an
expert reviewer who is familiar with the literature in this area
was supportive of the paper, I would be pleased to recommend
acceptance for presentation at the ICFP conference.

Specific comments:

p1, The abstract didn't (for me) give a clear overview of what the
paper was about; I had to also read the introduction to determine
this.  It would be beneficial to further revise/polish the abstract.

p1, The introductory section is a bit long at present, and would
benefit from being compresed to around 2/3 of a page.

p2, "unprecedented freedom" -> "freedom".

p4, In section 3 it would be useful to include a simple example,
e.g. by relating back to the rectangle example from section one.

The introduction to BiGUL specifically is in §4, in terms of the Hoare-style rules (i.e., axiomatic _semantics_) and aided by informal operational semantics. # Detailed responses (exceeding the 500 word limit, but in case the reviewers are interested)

## To RB

**“[L]ens-based approaches”:** We mean your first interpretation, and we say “approaches” (in the plural) to include lens combinator–based and bidirectionalisation approaches. (And yes, we’ll clarify this once we have space.)

**Theorem 3.8:** It is a tricky theorem, indeed. The way we understand it is that the precondition for put also determines the extent of get behaviour that we can be sure is constrained by the consistency relation, and thus, for understanding get behaviour, we’re incentivised to make the precondition as general as possible. The paper mentions the extreme case where the precondition is always true, in which case we can say plainly that get is a refinement of the consistency relation. In any case, we’re supposed to rely on Theorem 5.4 to fully understand get behaviour, not Theorem 3.8 (as explicitly mentioned by the paper below Theorem 3.8).

**`replace * replace`:** The assertion `{ _ (v, w) | v = w }` cannot be expressed as `{ (s, t) (v, w) | L s v ∧ R t w }` for any relations `L` and `R`, and thus the product rule is not applicable. Corresponding to the Agda formalisation, the assertions/relations are all strongly typed, and all values have canonical forms. So the source values referred to in the assertions you mention are all of a product type, and a value of a product type is necessarily a pair (there’s no ‘undefined’ value). Sorry for the confusion. (Mixing `_` and `(_, _)` here is probably not a good idea.)

**Exit conditions:** We’ve written up a very detailed explanation of the design of `case`, in particular the exit conditions, in our tutorial paper (Hu & Ko 2017); in a nutshell, exit conditions end up being pretty much the same as assertions in reversible programming, where assertions (which should be true _after_ branch execution) become conditions (which should be true _before_ branch execution) during inverse execution.

**`emb`:** On the other hand, in practice `emb` can be important when the programmer does not want to write a proper BiGUL program (like when it’s particularly difficult or when some part of the program needs to be quickly prototyped for testing), and thus deserves some attention regarding what properties it expects its arguments to satisfy — the “observations” are definitely useful. As for general applicability (usefulness) of the framework, the development of `keyAlign` is a representative example, and working through the development is enough to give a sense of the generality of the Hoare-style logic.

**Alignment:** This is where we say that alignment is representative. And we can deal with this representative example.

**Product rule:** We only mentioned that the star operator is related to separating conjunction, and didn’t say that our product rule is related to the frame rule.
