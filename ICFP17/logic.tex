\section{Theory of asymmetric lenses and putback triples}
\label{sec:putback-triples}

We start from a brief review of the theory of asymmetric lenses, but we state the facts directly in terms of \BiGUL.%
\todo{specific \BiGUL\ constructs to be introduced later}\
Every \BiGUL\ program denotes a \emph{well-behaved lens}, which consists of two transformations inverse to each other in a certain sense.

\begin{definition} \label{def:put-and-get}
\BiGUL\ programs operating on source type~|S| and view type~|V| are assigned the type |S <~ V|, and have two semantics:
\begin{code}
put  : (S <~ V) -> S -> V  -> Maybe S
get  : (S <~ V) -> S       -> Maybe V
\end{code}
The |put| --- or \emph{putback} --- semantics is also called the \emph{backward} semantics, and the |get| semantics is also called the \emph{forward} semantics.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[well-behavedness]
Any \BiGUL\ program~|b| satisfies the following two \emph{well-behavedness} properties, \emph{\ref{prop:PutGet}} and \emph{\ref{prop:GetPut}}:
\begin{align}
& |all(s, v, s')| \quad |put b s v = Just s'| \quad|=>|\quad \awa{|put|}{|get|}\;|b|\;\awa{|s v|}{|s'|}\;|= Just v| \tag{PutGet}\label{prop:PutGet} \\
& \awa{|all(s', s, v)|}{|all(s, v)|} \quad \awa{|put|}{|get|}\;\awa{|b s v|}{|b s|}\;|= Just|\;\awa{|s'|}{|v|} \quad|=>|\quad |put b s v = Just s| \tag{GetPut} \label{prop:GetPut}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

This theorem was proved by \citet{Ko-BiGUL} for the original \BiGUL; we have patched the proof of this theorem to work for the version of \BiGUL\ used in this paper.

\BiGUL\ is designed such that the \BiGUL\ programmer can think solely in the putback direction.
This is theoretically feasible because of the following theorem (originally \citet{Foster-thesis}'s Lemma~2.2.5), which says that once the programmer specifies a putback transformation, the corresponding forward transformation is uniquely determined.\todo{no ambiguity, comparison with get-based approach, etc}

\begin{theorem}[dominance of putback semantics] \label{thm:dominance}
Let |l, r : S <~ V|.
\[ \IF |put l = put r| \THEN |get l = get r| \PERIOD \]
\end{theorem}

Programming a putback transformation is comparable to programming with states:
the \BiGUL\ programmer is given a source state and a view state, and writes a \BiGUL\ program to manipulate the two states, with the aim of transferring all information in the view to the source.
Reasoning about \BiGUL\ programs thus consists of tracking the properties satisfied by the states at each step, and it should not come as a surprise that a Hoare-style logic is well suited for performing this kind of reasoning about states.
Before introducing Hoare-style triples for \BiGUL\ programs, let us fix our notation of relations (for specifying preconditions and postconditions).

\begin{notation}
Relations on types |A_1|\,, |A_2|\,, $\ldots,$ |A_n| are considered inhabitants of the type |POWER(A_1 TIMES A_2 TIMES {-"\cdots\;"-} TIMES A_n)|.
When a relation~|R| of this type relates elements |x_1 : A_1|\,, |x_2 : A_2|\,, $\ldots,$ |x_n : A_n|\,, we write |R x_1 x_2 ... x_n|\,. %(in a curried form, to save parentheses and commas).
\end{notation}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{putback triple} is a \BiGUL\ program |b : S <~ V| surrounded by two \emph{assertions}:
\[ |(ASSERT R) ~ b ~ (ASSERT R')| \]
where
\begin{itemize}
\item |R : (POWER(S TIMES V))| is the \emph{precondition} (on the original source and view), and
\item |R' : (POWER(S TIMES S TIMES V))| is the \emph{postcondition} (on the updated source, original source, and view).
\end{itemize}
Valid putback triples are inductively defined by the rules in \autoref{fig:putback-proof-rules} (which will be explained in \autoref{sec:putback-proof-rules}).
\end{definition}

The meaning of the putback triples is given by the following soundness theorem:

\begin{theorem}[soundness of putback triples] \label{thm:putback-soundness}
Let |b : S <~ V|, |R : (POWER(S TIMES V))|, and |R' : (POWER(S TIMES S TIMES V))|.
\[ \IF |(ASSERT R) ~ b ~ (ASSERT R')| \THEN |(all(s, v)) ~ R s v ~ => ~ (some(s')) ~ put b s v = Just s' ~ && ~ R' s' s v| \PERIOD \]
\end{theorem}

In prose: a valid putback triple |(ASSERT R) b (ASSERT R')| guarantees that if the original source and the view satisfies the precondition~|R|, then |put b| will successfully produce an updated source satisfying the postcondition~|R'|, which can relate the updated source to the original source and the view.
This theorem is proved by induction on the proof rules, as usual.

\autoref{thm:dominance} tells us that putback behaviour completely determines get behaviour.
On the other hand, putback triples are about putback behaviour --- shouldn't they tell us something about get behaviour as well?
This is indeed the case, as will be shown by \autoref{thm:partial-forward-consistency}.
Its statement will make use of some additional definitions and notational conventions, which will also be used throughout the rest of this paper.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{comprehension relation} of type |(POWER(A_1 TIMES A_2 TIMES {-"\cdots\,"-} TIMES A_n))| has the form
\[ |COM(pat_1 pat_2 ... pat_n || prop)| \]
where each |pat_i| is a pattern for elements of type~|A_i| and |prop| is a proposition that can refer to the variables in the patterns.
The patterns we use in the paper include variables, constants, constructors, and the wildcard pattern~(`|_|').
The relation holds for |x_1 : A_1|, $\ldots,$ |x_n : A_n| exactly when each |x_i| matches |pat_i| and |prop| holds after substituting the matched components for the corresponding pattern variables.
\end{definition}

\begin{notation}
We usually omit the proposition part of a comprehension relation when the proposition is trivially true, keeping only the pattern part.
For example, |COM((_ :: _))| holds exactly for non-empty lists, and |COM(_ _)| is the always true binary relation.
\end{notation}

\begin{notation}
An assertion whose body is a comprehension relation, like |(ASSERT(COM(pat || prop)))|, is usually written with the angle brackets omitted, like |(ASSERT(pat || prop))|.
\end{notation}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{graph} of a function |f : A -> Maybe B| is a relation |GRAPH f : (POWER(A TIMES B))| which relates |a : A| and |b : B| exactly when |f a = Just b|.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[partial forward consistency] \label{thm:partial-forward-consistency}
Let |b : S <~ V|, |R : (POWER(S TIMES V))|, and |R' : (POWER(S TIMES V))|.
\[ \IF |(ASSERT R) ~ b ~ (ASSERT(s' _ v || R' s' v))| \THEN |GRAPH(get b) CAP R ~ SUBSETEQ ~ R'| \PERIOD \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose |get b s = Just v| and |R s v|.
The latter triggers \autoref{thm:putback-soundness}, so we know that |put b s v = Just s'| for some~|s'| and that |R' s' v| holds.
On the other hand, by \ref{prop:GetPut}, we can turn |get b s = Just v| into |put b s v = Just s|.
Seeing that |put b s v| computes to both |s'|~and~|s|, we can deduce |s' = s|, and thus having |R' s' v| is the same as having |R' s v|.
\end{proof}

That is, if we can prove that a putback transformation establishes consistency~|R'| between the updated source and the view, then, roughly speaking, a part of the behaviour of the corresponding forward transformation will be constrained by~|R'|.
We call \autoref{thm:partial-forward-consistency} \emph{partial} forward consistency for two reasons:
The first reason is that \autoref{thm:partial-forward-consistency} does not guarantee that the entire graph of the forward transformation will be contained in~|R'| --- the containment is guaranteed only for the part of the graph that falls within~|R|.
In practice, this makes \autoref{thm:partial-forward-consistency} not very helpful unless |R|~is trivially true, in which case the entire graph will indeed be guaranteed to be contained in~|R'|.
Even in this case, though, \autoref{thm:partial-forward-consistency} still says nothing about the totality/partiality of the forward transformation, i.e., on which subset of input sources the forward transformation can successfully produce results, and this is the second reason.
We will augment \autoref{thm:partial-forward-consistency} to get a practically useful version, which will be \autoref{thm:total-forward-consistency}.
But before that, let us look at the concrete putback proof rules and some examples of putback reasoning.

\subsection{Atomic constructs}

\BiGUL\ has three atomic constructs, whose corresponding rules are in the first row of \autoref{fig:putback-proof-rules}.
\begin{itemize}
\item The |fail| construct has type |S <~ V| for any |S|~and~|V|.
The precondition of the |fail| rule is the empty relation~|EMPTY|, saying that no input can make |fail| compute successfully.
\item The |replace| construct has type |S <~ S| for any~|S|, and replaces the source with the view; correspondingly, the postcondition states that the updated source~|s'| will be equal to the view~|v|.
\item The |skip| construct takes a function |f : S -> V| as an argument and has type |S <~ V|.
It ignores the view and leaves the source as it is; correspondingly, the postcondition states that the updated source~|s'| will be equal to the original source~|s|.
Unlike |replace|, we cannot |skip| under all circumstances --- before throwing the view away, we must ensure that it can be recovered from the source, or otherwise there is no hope to establish \ref{prop:PutGet}.
The precondition thus requires that the view can be computed from the source by~|f|.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Product}

Given two \BiGUL\ programs |l : S <~ V| and |r : T <~ W|, we can form the product of the two programs |l * r : (S TIMES T) <~ (V TIMES W)|.
If two putback triples with preconditions |L|~and~|R| have been established for |l|~and~|r|, the precondition of the product program will be |L * R = (COM((s, t) (v, w) || L s v && R t w))|; its postcondition is defined analogously.
This relation-level `|*|' operator can (indeed) be seen as a simple variant of separating conjunction~\citep{Reynolds-separation-logic}, constructing a relation on pairs from two relations respectively on the first and second components.

\begin{example}[parallel replacement] \label{ex:replace-squared}
We can now construct simple derivations like the following one for |replace * replace|:
\begin{code}
(ASSERT((_ , _) (_ , _)))
   (ASSERT(_ _))
   replace
   (ASSERT(s' _ v | s' = v))
*  (ASSERT(_ _))
   replace
   (ASSERT(t' _ w | t' = w))
(ASSERT((s', t') (_ , _) (v , w) | s' = v && t' = w))
\end{code}
First, note that the syntax tree structure of |replace * replace| is reflected in indentation: the top-level node is~`|*|', whose two sub-nodes --- both being |replace| --- are indented to the next level.
Then, following the indentation structure, the assertions are added: the assertions about a node are put on the same indentation level as the node, with the precondition and postcondition appearing respectively before and after the node.
This format is compact and yet retains the tree structure of the derivation, making it easier to check the correctness of the derivation.
\end{example}

\subsection{The consequence rule}

The consequence rule we adopt (the right one in the second row in \autoref{fig:putback-proof-rules}) may seem unusual, but first note that it is a stronger version of the usual one:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{l}
|T ~ SUBSETEQ ~ R| \rulepsep
|{-"\assert{\awa{T}{R}}\;"-} ~ b ~ {-"\;\assert{\awa{T^\prime}{R^\prime}}"-}| \rulepsep
|R' ~ SUBSETEQ ~ T'| \\ \hline
\phantom{|T ~ SUBSETEQ ~ R| \rulepsep}|{-"\assert{T}\;"-} ~ b ~ {-"\;\assert{T^\prime}"-}|
\end{array}
\label{eq:usual-consequence-rule}
\end{equation}
To see why we need a stronger consequence rule, consider deriving this triple:
\[ |(ASSERT(_ (v, w) || v = w)) ~ replace * replace ~ (ASSERT((s', t') _ _ || s' = t'))| \]
where there is some entanglement between the first and second components in the precondition and postcondition, so the product rule is not directly applicable.
We can try to extend the derivation in Example~\ref{ex:replace-squared} using the usual consequence rule~(\ref{eq:usual-consequence-rule}):
\begin{code}
(ASSERT(_ (v, w) | v = w))
(ASSERT((_ , _) (_ , _)))
replace * replace
(ASSERT((s', t') (_ , _) (v , w) | s' = v && t' = w))
VDOTS
(ASSERT((s', t') _ _ | s' = t'))
\end{code}
Adjacent assertions on the same indentation level indicate an invocation of the consequence rule, with the one above implying the one below.
In this derivation, there is one such invocation, which turns |(COM(_ (v , w) || v = w))| into |(COM((_ , _) (_ , _)))| so that the product rule can apply.
On the other hand, the postcondition that we can establish, namely |(COM((s', t') (_ , _) (v, w) || s' = v && t' = w))|, does not imply the postcondition we want to establish, namely |(COM((s', t') _ _ || s' = t'))| (hence the vertical dots separating the two postconditions in the derivation, to avoid suggesting that the consequence rule is invoked).
The usual consequence rule~(\ref{eq:usual-consequence-rule}) can help us to get rid of the entanglement |v = w|, but that entanglement is needed for establishing the final implication (since |s' = v = w = t'|).
We thus need the stronger consequence rule in \autoref{fig:putback-proof-rules} to be able to carry over whatever we know about the original source and the view from the precondition to the postcondition.
When working in our derivation format, the stronger consequence rule allows us to prove an implication between adjacent postconditions using whichever preconditions on the same indentation level as additional premises.

\subsection{Rearrangement}

The product combinator allows us to synchronise source and view tuples of arbitrary size, provided that their structures are the same.
When this is not the case, in \BiGUL\ we can use a ``simple'' pattern-matching $\lambda$-expression to rearrange the source and/or the view so that their structures match and are ready for further synchronisation.
For example, the first height-keeping strategy we proposed for the rectangle width updating problem in \autoref{sec:introduction} can be expressed in \BiGUL\ as:
\begin{code}
keepHeight  : (Nat , Nat) <~ Nat
keepHeight  =  rearrV v -> (v, ())
                    replace
                 *  skip const ()
\end{code}
Initially, the view is a single number, whereas the source is a pair.
To make their structures match, we use the view rearrangement operation |rearrV v -> (v, ())| to apply the $\lambda$-expression |\ v -> (v, ())| to the view and use the result as the new view.
Inside |rearrV|, the source and view are both pairs now, so we can use |replace * skip const ()| to replace the width with the view and keep the height as it is.
Below we will mainly discuss view rearrangement; source rearrangement is largely analogous, and will be briefly discussed towards the end of this subsection.

The general form of view rearrangement is |rearrV vpat -> wpat INNER b : S <~ V|, where |vpat| is a pattern for the original view type~|V|, |wpat| is a ``pattern'' for a new view type~|W|, and the inner program~|b| has type |S <~ W|.
(The symbol~`|INNER|' indicates that |b|~is syntactically a sub-node of `|rearrV vpat -> wpat|'.)
Strictly speaking, |wpat| is not a pattern but an expression, which can consist of variables in |vpat|, constants, and constructors.
(Apart from the fact that |wpat| looks similar to a pattern, we will explain why it is beneficial to think of |wpat| as a pattern shortly.)
Wildcards are not allowed in |vpat|, and all variables in |vpat| must appear in |wpat| and can appear multiple times --- these synctactic restrictions ensure that the $\lambda$-expression does not lose information (and can be inverted).

Intuitively, a rearrangement only massages the state into an alternate shape suitable for further processing, rather than applying an arbitrary and distorting transformation.
This intuition significantly influences the design of the rearrangement rules, which suggest that rearrangement is essentially just a ``change of perspective''.
If we are rearranging the view from |vpat| to |wpat|, it must mean that the view matches |vpat| right before the rearrangement, and we should be able to state properties satisfied by the view at that point in terms of its components.
The precondition for |rearrV| is thus a comprehension relation |(COM(s vpat || R s (ENV(vpat))))|, which requires that the view matches |vpat| and that any properties about the view is stated in terms of the variables in |vpat|, which we denote by |(ENV(vpat))|.
After the rearrangement, the new view will retain all the components of the original view; the components may be shuffled around and duplicated, but whatever we knew about the components will remain true.
The precondition for the inner program~|b| is thus |(COM(s wpat || R s (ENV(wpat))))|, asserting that the new view matches |wpat| and whatever holds for |(ENV(vpat))| also holds for |(ENV(wpat))|.
This is why it is beneficial to think of |wpat| also as a pattern, even though that means in general we have to allow non-linear patterns, where multiple occurrences of the same variable indicate implicitly that values at those positions should be equal.
When using the rule in actual derivations, the two preconditions will differ only in their view patterns and have the same proposition part, since the syntactic restriction on |rearrV| implies |(ENV(wpat)) = (ENV(vpat))|,
Therefore, the view rearrangement rule only changes the shape we expect the view to take, not the properties we know about the content of the view.
This change-of-perspective interpretation works for the postconditions as well.

Source rearrangement is analogous to view rearrangement.
The general form is |rearrS spat -> tpat INNER b : S <~ V| where |spat| is a pattern for the original source type~|S|, |tpat| is a pattern for the new source type~|T|, and the inner program~|b| has type |T <~ V|.
The same syntactic restrictions apply to |spat| and |tpat|.
Operationally, the source is transformed using |\ spat -> tpat|, and |b| is executed on the new source and the view; after that, the updated source must match |tpat|, and will be transformed back to the shape of |spat| (as if evaluating |\ spat -> tpat| backwards).
This operational intuition is faithfully captured in the source rearrangement rule, which still suggests a change of perspective.
In particular, the postcondition for~|b| says clearly that the updated source produced by~|b| should match |tpat|, a requirement often overlooked by novice \BiGUL\ programmers.
(The pattern |tpat'| is just |tpat| with its variables freshly renamed, to avoid name clashes with the other occurrence of |tpat| for the original source.)

Finally, whereas the rearrangement rules we have presented rely essentially on pattern-matching comprehension relations, the actual \Agda\ formalisation takes a different approach and avoids modelling pattern-matching comprehension relations entirely.
The formalisation is sketched here for the curious reader.
We build on \citet{Ko-BiGUL}'s formalisation of pattern matching, which uses heavily indexed types to guarantee various kinds of safety.
To avoid introducing the heavy \Agda\ notation below, we will give a simplified account within the specific scenario of rearranging the view from |vpat| for type~|V| to |wpat| for type~|W|.
The actual formalisation first defines a type |PatResult|: an inhabitant of |PatResult| is the result of matching a value of type~|V| with |vpat|, and can be thought of as a mapping from every variable in |vpat| to the component matching the variable.
Two relations are then defined: |Match : (POWER(V TIMES PatResult))| relates |v : V| and |r : PatResult| exactly when |v|~matches |vpat| and |r|~is the result, and |Eval : (POWER(PatResult TIMES W))| relates |r : PatResult| and |w : W| exactly when |w|~is the result of evaluating |wpat| (as an expression) using |r|~as the environment.
With these two relations, we state the precondition for the whole |rearrV| program as |(COM(s v || (some(r)) Match v r && R s r))|, and the precondition for the inner program as |(COM(s w || (some(r)) Eval r w && R s r))|, making no use of pattern-matching comprehension relations.

\section{Theory of range triples}
\label{sec:range-triples}

\begin{theorem}[soundness of range triples] \label{thm:range-soundness}
Let |b : S <~ V|, |R : (POWER(S TIMES V))|, and |P' : (POWER S)|.
\[ \IF |(ASSERTRANGE R) ~ b ~ (ASSERTRANGE P')| \THEN |(all(s)) ~ P' s ~ => ~ (some(v)) ~ get b s = Just v ~ && ~ R s v| \PERIOD \]
\end{theorem}

Thanks to well-behavedness, we can rewrite the right-hand side of \autoref{thm:range-soundness} into a statement primarily about |put|:

\begin{lemma}
The conclusion of \autoref{thm:range-soundness} is equivalent to
\[ (|(all(s')) ~ P' s' ~ => ~ (some(s, v)) ~ put b s v = Just s' ~ && ~ R s v|) \quad|&&|\quad |(GRAPH(get b)) CAP (COM(s _ || P' s)) ~ SUBSETEQ ~ R| \]
\end{lemma}

The left conjunct is the primary way in which we think about these new triples: if |(ASSERTRANGE R) b (ASSERTRANGE P')| can be derived, then the \emph{range} of updated sources produced by applying |put b| to those inputs satisfying~|R| will be at least~|P'|.
Hence the name \emph{range triples}.
The right conjunct says that there is a ``side effect'' when we think about these triples in the putback direction: the subset of inputs considered will be forced to include those related by |get| with its domain restricted to~|P'|.
So, for example, we will not be able to deduce |(ASSERTRANGE(m n || m = n+1)) replace (ASSERTRANGE(_))| even though the left conjunct is true for this pair of |R|~and~|P'|.
This ``side effect'' normally does not get in the way, though, since the preconditions we specify are normally larger than the consistency relations, which in turn contain the |get| functions.

\begin{theorem}[total forward consistency] \label{thm:total-forward-consistency}
\[ |(ASSERT R) ~ b ~ (ASSERT(s' _ v || R' s' v))| \quad|&&|\quad |(ASSERTRANGE R) ~ b ~ (ASSERTRANGE P)| \quad|=>|\quad |(all(s)) P s => (some(v)) get b s = Just v && R' s v| \]
\end{theorem}

We will write
\[ |(ASSERT R) ~ b ~ (ASSERT R') (ASSERTRANGE P)| \quad\text{as an abbreviation of}\quad |(ASSERT R) ~ b ~ (ASSERT R') ~ ~ && ~ ~ (ASSERTRANGE R) ~ b ~ (ASSERTRANGE P)| \]

\section{Verifying key-based alignment}
\label{sec:key-based-alignment}

Several variants of alignment have been implemented in \BiGUL~\citep{Zan-Brul, Mendes-delta-alignment}.
Here we choose to verify a variant that is non-trivial and yet not overly complicated: key-based alignment.

For now, \citet{Barbosa-matching-lenses}'s matching lenses may be more sophisticated, but they are special-purpose and require the invention of a new framework in which everything is built from scratch.
Instead, we strive to implement all lenses from just a fixed set of primitives, aiming to promote bidirectional programming to the same status as general-purpose programming.

\begin{code}
keyAlign : Eq K => (S -> K) -> (V -> K) -> (S <~ V) -> (V -> S) -> ([S] <~ [V])
keyAlign ks kv b c =
  case
    normal [] [] exit []
      rearrV [] -> ()
         skip const ()
    normal (s :: _) (v :: _) | ks s == kv v exit (_ :: _)
      rearrS (s :: ss) -> (s, ss)
        rearrV (v :: vs) -> (v, vs)
             b
          *  keyAlign ks kv b c
    adaptive (_ :: _) []
      \ _ _ -> []
    adaptive ss (v :: _) | kv v `elem` map ks ss
      \ ss (v :: _) -> extract ks kv v ss
    adaptive _ (_ :: _)
      \ ss (v :: _) -> c v :: ss
\end{code}

\begin{code}
extract : Eq K => (S -> K) -> (V -> K) -> V -> [S] -> [S]
extract ks kv v []         = []
extract ks kv v (s :: ss)  | ks s == kv v  =  s :: ss
                           | otherwise     =  case extract ks kv v ss of
                                                []         -> s :: []
                                                s' :: ss'  -> s' :: s :: ss'
\end{code}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A proper characterisation of the behaviour of composition will be essential for avoiding problems about composition as discussed by, e.g., \citet[Section~2.2]{Diskin-delta-asymmetric}.
\[ \begin{array}{l}
|(ASSERT (a b' || (some(b, c)) R a c && R' a b && U' b' b c)) ~ l ~ (ASSERT(COM(a' _ b || R' a' b) CAP T') (ASSERTRANGE P)| \\
|(ASSERT (b c || (some(a)) R' a b && R a c)) ~ r ~ (ASSERT U')|
\\ \hline
|(ASSERT(R CAP (COM(a _ || P a)))) ~ l . r ~ (ASSERT(a' a c || (some(b, b')) T' a' a b' && U' b' b c ))|
\end{array} \]
This requires the putback and range triples to be defined mutually inductively.
%A mutually inductive treatment can also shift the disjointness requirement to the ranges of normal branches instead of their exit conditions, allowing the exit conditions to be specified trivially, which can have a serious impact on runtime efficiency in the forward direction, but can be helpful when prototypic or experimental programs need to be developed without too much time for careful reasoning.

It has been realised~\citep[Section~4.1]{Stevens-QVT}\todo{some other citations}\ that, for practical bidirectional applications, a declarative approach --- in the sense that the programmer writes down only a consistency relation and relies on the system to derive a well-behaved but otherwise arbitrary implementation --- is hardly enough.
The BX community currently concentrates on the exploration of more forms of well-behavedness properties, but we should not be satisfied with only well-behavedness guarantees; instead, we should also start aiming to precisely characterise the behaviour of bidirectional programs, treating bidirectional programming seriously as we treat general-purpose programming.

We need general and formal reasoning principles for bidirectional programming to make it become a proper discipline of programming.

Programming languages should be shipped with reasoning principles; even domain-specific languages deserve domain-specific reasoning principles!

\textit{Why isn't the title of this paper ``An Axiomatic Basis for \emph{Putback-Based} Bidirectional Programming''?}
Within the realm of asymmetric lenses, the get-based approach is fundamentally just about \emph{programming consistency specifications}; only with the putback-based approach can we start talking about \emph{programming to meet functional specifications}, and only in this case does an axiomatic semantics make sense.

\textit{Doesn't \autoref{thm:total-forward-consistency} say that we need to reason in both directions anyway?}
Even if the sceptic reader insists that constructing the range triples requires thinking in the forward direction, they will still have to admit that, according to \autoref{thm:total-forward-consistency}, what needs to be verified in the forward direction is normally much easier than proving that |get| is contained in the consistency relation. This is because the major work has been done in the backward direction.

Completeness has not been proved, but we have shown that the logic works for various examples. Relationship with constructive continuity.

\BiGUL\ is stateful, but only in the ``Reader'' way, in particular using the Reader monad's `|local|' operation to modify the current source and view.
Our Hoare-style logic can be said to be merely tracking how the current source and view are manipulated by implicit `|local|' operations.

Towards a syntactic treatment for automation.

It has been argued that ``putback'' is the essence of bidirectional programming~\citep{Fischer-essence-of-BX}.
We would like to amend this statement: Putback-based \emph{reasoning} is the essence of bidirectional programming.
This paper has shown, with the Hoare-style logic, what such reasoning can look like.
